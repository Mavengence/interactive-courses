The five-act structure

Every data system design interview, regardless of company, is the same five acts. Use this as a template. If the interviewer doesn't lead you through them, lead yourself.

  1. Clarify (5 min). Don't draw anything yet. Ask what the system is for, who reads the output, what scale, what freshness, what consistency. Write the answers on the board — they're the spec. The three numbers to always extract: writes/sec, reads/sec, freshness target.
  2. Skeleton (5 min). Draw the six layers from lesson 1. Mark which layers the question touches. Identify the dominant constraint (usually one of: latency, scale, correctness, or cost).
  3. Deep dive (20 min). Pick the hardest 1-2 layers and design them in detail. Schema, partition key, processing topology, SLO targets. This is where the interview is decided. For storage: name the partition key and explain your choice. For streaming: name the watermark strategy. For serving: explain what's on the read path and what's pre-computed.
  4. Failure modes (10 min). What breaks? Late data, hot partitions, schema drift, backfills, region failover. Name the monitoring signal for each. The interviewer will probe; surface it yourself first.
  5. Trade-offs & next steps (5 min). Recap what you optimized for. Name what you traded away (consistency? cost? simplicity?). Mention what you'd do next quarter — multi-region, GDPR compliance, cost optimization — to show you see past the 45-minute window.

The single most common mistake: starting to design before clarifying. You'll draw an expensive real-time system when a nightly batch would have been fine. Two minutes of clarifying questions can save twenty minutes of design rework in a 45-minute interview.

Phrases that read as senior

These are not magic words. They're the verbal markers of someone who's actually built the systems before. Use them when they're true:

  • "Before we design this, what does the consumer of the output actually need?" — Frames the problem in terms of read patterns, not write patterns.
  • "What's the cost of this being one minute late versus one hour late?" — Surfaces the freshness SLO instead of assuming it.
  • "Let me size this before I pick a tool." — 1B events/day at 1KB each is 12KB/sec average, 120KB/sec peak. That's tiny. Many problems are smaller than they look once you do the math.
  • "I'd default to Iceberg + dbt + Airflow unless we have a specific reason not to." — Names a sensible boring default. Boring defaults are senior.
  • "The risk here is X. The mitigation is Y. The accepted residual risk is Z." — Trade-off literacy. Also, naming residual risk explicitly is a thing only senior engineers do.
  • "I'd want to see a prototype before committing to that." — Refusing to over-design when uncertainty is high.

Phrases that read as junior

  • "We'd use Kafka." (without saying why)
  • "I'd just use machine learning to handle that."
  • "It needs to be exactly-once." (when the sink is an HTTP API)
  • "I'd put it all in Snowflake." (when scale is unstated)
  • "Let me just draw it." (without clarifying first)
  • "That edge case is unlikely." (when asked about a failure mode)

None of these are wrong, exactly. They're all under-specified — they skip the reasoning step that an IC5 is hired for. The fix is the same in all cases: name the assumption you're making out loud, and explain when you'd revisit it.

One last move

At the end of the interview, when they ask "any questions for us?" — ask "what's the data quality story like here?" and "how do you handle backfills today?"

You'll get a real answer that tells you whether you'd actually want the job. And the interviewer will note that you cared about the operational reality, not the architecture diagram.
